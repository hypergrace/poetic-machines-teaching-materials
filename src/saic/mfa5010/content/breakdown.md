To pass this class, you will need to engage deeply with texts outside of class, in discussion, and in your artistic practice. Your classroom participation and the assignments for this class will be opportunities for you to demonstrate this.

These texts are layered and extremely challenging. Many spend years of decades with them; they become old friends. Like friendship, even after decades of intimate exchange, there is no goal, completion criteria, or mastery in reading these texts. Certain conceptual moves and themes become more important and salient and others will fade away. 

We have a mere fifteen weeks to spend with these texts; your patience is required. In some moments, you may expect to be in a state of exalted or frenzied confusion, like being adrift in a dark carnival intoxicated by some shimmering, ephemeral cohesion. In others you will feel and think a concept with clarity, having arrived onto a plane of abstraction whereby what you had grasped at before grasps you back. Sometimes you just be fucking confused. In those moments, show up for yourself, show up for the text. As Brian Massumi writes in the translator's introduction to A Thousand Plateaus, you can read it like jazz, play the concepts like a record.       

I have designed these assignments both to give you a taste of deep engagement with the art of concept creation and to create onramps for your engaging with those concepts through experimental artistic practice. Here is the breakdown; there is more information on the assignments in the "assignment" tab of the course website.

- 40% concept map
- 20% practice luring theory presentation
- 40% chaosmotic experimentations

In this class, you are responsible to find personal and artistic source of inspiration and excitement that animate your engagement with these texts. You will find that sharing that excitement in class will reward and encourage your classmates; you will get the most out of what you share if it is crafted to invite engagement from your peers, not mere approval from your instructor. 

I'm talking about an experience of collective enjoyment. To that end, participation in the classroom looks different for different people. Some work through understanding in classroom dialogue. Others think and feel actively without speaking. Some draw. Find a way to share this with others, and, challenge yourself to process these ideas differently than you usually do. The seminar is a laboratory; try something out.
